Electric charge

Three parts of readings and quizzes · PHYS1025

1. What's inside everything

Read this first. Everything else depends on it.

Everything around you — the desk, the air, your hand — is made of tiny building blocks called atoms.

Each atom has a heavy centre called the nucleus, packed with particles called protons. Protons carry a positive charge, and they are stuck. They never leave.

Around the outside are much lighter particles called electrons. They carry a negative charge, and some of them are loose enough to move away.

Normally an atom has the same number of each, so the positive and negative cancel out. We call that neutral.

When something becomes charged, only one thing has happened: electrons moved. Nothing was created and nothing was destroyed.

Notice what neutral does not mean. A neutral object is not empty of charge. It is absolutely packed with charge — there is just exactly as much positive as negative, so the two cancel.

2. Charges push and pull

Charges do not touch each other, but they still feel each other across empty space.

The rule is short: the same signs push apart, opposite signs pull together.

Like charges repel. Opposite charges attract.

This is why a positively charged rod pulls electrons out of a neutral object when you touch them together — the positive rod is attracting the negative electrons.

3. Numbers in physics

The part that costs the most marks. Worth twenty minutes.

Physics uses very big and very small numbers, so we write them in a short form.

10−9 simply means divide by a thousand million. Rather than write that out every time, we put a letter in front of the unit.

So 12 nC means 12 × 10−9 C. The letter is doing all the work.

Change everything into metres and coulombs before you put any numbers into a formula. Not after. Not halfway.

4. The three ways to charge something

All three do the same thing — move electrons. They differ only in how.

By rubbing

Rub two materials together and electrons are scraped from one onto the other. One ends up short of electrons (positive), the other ends up with extra (negative).

By conduction — they touch

A charged object touches a neutral one. Electrons move across the contact until the charge is shared. Both objects end up with the same sign.

By induction — no touching

Hold a charged object close without touching. Electrons inside the neutral object shift, so one end goes negative and the other positive. The object as a whole is still neutral — nothing has entered or left it.

Charged by rubbing or induction → the two objects end up with opposite signs. Charged by conduction → they end up with the same sign.

5. One charge, one arrow

The building block. Everything else is this, repeated.

A single charge fills the space around it with an electric field. At any point, the field is an arrow — it has a size and a direction.

The size comes from one formula:

E = kQ / r2   with k = 9.0 × 109

Use the size of the charge here. Ignore the minus sign on a negative charge — it does a different job.

The sign decides the direction instead:

A worked number. What is the field 2.0 cm from a charge of +3.0 nC?

Convert first: r = 0.020 m, so r2 = 4.0 × 10−4 m2, and Q = 3.0 × 10−9 C.

E = (9.0 × 109)(3.0 × 10−9) / (4.0 × 10−4) = 6.8 × 104 N/C, pointing away from the charge.

6. Two charges on a line

With two charges you work out each field separately, then combine them. On a straight line that is easy — the two arrows either point the same way or opposite ways.

Here is the part that catches people out:

Opposite charges make the fields add between them. Like charges make them subtract. It feels backwards, so check it on the picture every time.

The reason is simple once you see it. Between a + and a −, "away from the positive" and "toward the negative" point the same way. Between two positives, each field points away from its own charge — so they fight.

Where the field is zero

Between two like charges there is a point where the two arrows are exactly equal and cancel. To find it, set the two sizes equal and solve:

kQ1 / x2 = kQ2 / (d − x)2

Cancel the k, take the square root of both sides straight away, and you avoid a messy quadratic. The point always sits closer to the smaller charge — a useful check.

7. Arrows at an angle

When the charges are not in a straight line, the arrows point at angles and you cannot just add or subtract the numbers.

The trick is to replace each slanted arrow with two arrows — one across and one up — that together take you to the same place.

Then the whole problem becomes two easy sums:

Add up all the across parts. Add up all the up parts. Those two answers are your components.

Very often one of the two sums comes out as zero, because the arrangement is symmetric and the parts cancel in pairs. Spotting that saves you most of the work — and if a question is worth fewer marks than its twin, that is usually the one that cancels.

Which part is which

Measure the angle from a line you choose, then stick to it. If the angle is measured from the vertical, the up part is E cos θ and the across part is E sin θ. Mark the angle on your own diagram before choosing — the part next to the angle always takes the cosine.

8. Getting the distance right

Half the marks lost in these questions are lost here, not in the physics.

The formula needs r2, and r is the distance from the charge to the point you care about. In shapes, that distance is rarely the number printed on the diagram.

For a square of side a, the diagonal is a√2, so from a corner to the centre is half of that: a/√2. For a = 10 cm that is 0.0707 m, and r2 = 5.0 × 10−3 m2.

For an equilateral triangle of side a, every corner is a distance a from every other corner. No square roots needed.

Find r, convert it to metres, then square it. Write r2 down as its own line of working before you touch the field formula.

9. The field between two plates

A completely different kind of field from the ones in part 2 — and a much easier one.

Take two flat metal plates, face to face, and connect them to a battery. One goes positive, the other negative, and the space between them fills with field.

What makes this special: the field between the plates is the same everywhere — same size, same direction, at every point in the gap. We call that a uniform field, and we draw it as evenly spaced parallel arrows.

The size of that field comes from one short formula:

E = V / d   — volts across the gap, divided by the gap in metres

A worked number. A potential difference of 5 kV across plates 50 mm apart.

Convert both: 5 kV = 5000 V, and 50 mm = 0.050 m.

E = 5000 / 0.050 = 1.0 × 105 N/C.

Notice there is no kQ/r2 anywhere here, and no distance squared. Plates are the easy case — the moment you see two plates and a voltage, reach for V/d.

10. When a charge hangs in balance

The single most common calculation in this topic. Learn the shape of it once.

Hang a small charged ball on a thread inside a sideways field and it will not hang straight down. The field pushes it sideways, so the thread settles at an angle and stays there.

Staying there is the whole key. Nothing is moving, so all the forces must cancel. Three forces act:

Split the tension into its up part and its sideways part. Up must balance the weight, sideways must balance the electric force:

T cos θ = mg   (vertical)      T sin θ = qE   (horizontal)

Now divide the second by the first. The T cancels and disappears completely:

tan θ = qE / mg   →   q = mg tan θ / E

That cancellation is the whole trick. You are never told the tension and you never need it. Any question that hangs a charge on a thread is this one formula with different numbers.

And the direction tells you the sign: if the ball leans the same way the field points, the charge is positive. If it leans against the field, it is negative.

11. Grams, millimetres and g

Ten marks have never been lost to Gauss's law. They are lost here.

These questions hand you numbers in whatever units suit the sentence, and every formula expects kilograms, metres and volts. Convert before you start, on their own lines of working.

Mass is not weight. m is in kilograms; mg is a force in newtons. The formula wants mg.

And do not panic when a number goes unused. If a question gives a 20.0 cm string and your working never touches it, that is fine — the length would only matter if you were asked how far sideways the ball moved. Forcing every number into the answer is how you end up with nonsense.

12. Gauss's law

This is bookwork. It is written out the same way every year, so learn the words and the picture.

Picture any closed surface — a bag, a box, a balloon — sitting in an electric field. Flux is a measure of how much field passes out through that surface.

Gauss's law says something remarkable: the total flux out depends on nothing but the charge inside. Not the shape of the surface, not its size, not where the charge sits inside it.

Φ = ∮ E · dA = Qenclosed / ε0   with ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12

By itself that looks useless, because the integral is frightening. The power comes from choosing the surface cleverly.

For a single point charge, choose a sphere centred on the charge. Two things then become true at once, and they are the two things the examiner wants you to say:

• By symmetry, E has the same size at every point on that sphere.
• E points straight out, so it is perpendicular to the surface everywhere.

Because E is constant and perpendicular, the integral collapses into a multiplication — field times area:

∮ E · dA = E × 4πr2

Set that equal to Q/ε0 and make E the subject:

E (4πr2) = Q / ε0   →   E = Q / (4πε0r2) = kQ / r2

And out drops Coulomb's law. The k you have been using all along is just a shorthand: k = 1/(4πε0) = 9.0 × 109.

13. What a capacitor stores

The biggest topic on the paper. More marks ride on these four readings than on anything else in the course.

A capacitor is just two conductors with a gap between them. Push charge onto one and an equal, opposite charge appears on the other, and a potential difference builds up across the gap.

How much charge it holds per volt is its capacitance:

C = Q / V   — measured in farads (F)

A farad is enormous, so real capacitors come in microfarads (µF, 10−6), nanofarads (nF, 10−9) and picofarads (pF, 10−12).

Here is the part that trips people. C does not depend on Q or V. Double the charge and the voltage doubles too, leaving C unchanged. Capacitance is fixed by the shape of the thing — how big the plates are and how far apart.

Bigger plates → more capacitance. Closer plates → more capacitance. A dielectric in the gap → K times more capacitance.

ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12, the same constant from Gauss's law. The dielectric constant K is just a number — 4 means four times the capacitance of the same capacitor with air in it.

14. Series and parallel

Two rules, and one thing to remember about each.

Everything in a capacitor network comes from asking a single question about each pair: what do they share?

Capacitors are backwards from resistors. Resistors add in series; capacitors add in parallel.

That is not an arbitrary fact to memorise. Two capacitors side by side in parallel are literally one capacitor with twice the plate area, and more area means more capacitance. Two in series make the total gap wider, and a wider gap means less.

When one capacitor is really two in parallel

Slide a slab of dielectric in so it covers only part of the plate area, but fills the gap from top to bottom. You now have two capacitors side by side — and because both pieces sit between the same two plates, both have the same p.d. across them.

Same voltage means parallel. Work out each piece from its own share of the area, then add.

Watch the trap: the slab's thickness here equals the full gap, so it is not splitting the capacitor top-from-bottom. It is splitting it side-by-side, by area. Check which one a question means before you start.

15. Energy in a capacitor

Charging a capacitor takes work. You are pushing charge onto a plate that is already charged and pushing back, so each extra bit costs more than the last. That work is stored in the field between the plates, and you get it back when it discharges.

Because Q and V rise together in a straight line, the stored energy is the triangle underneath — half of Q times V, not the whole rectangle. Substituting Q = CV gives three versions of the same formula:

U = ½QV = ½CV2 = Q2 / 2C

They always give the same answer, so pick whichever matches the two things you already know. Know C and V? Use ½CV2. Know Q and C — which is the usual case after charge has been shared around? Use Q2/2C.

The ½ is the most commonly dropped symbol on the whole paper. It costs a mark every time.

16. Sharing charge when the battery is removed

Read this one twice. It is worth more marks than anything else in part 4.

Everything hangs on one question: is the battery still connected?

• Battery connected. The battery holds the voltage fixed. Change the capacitor and the charge is what adjusts.
• Battery removed. The charge is now stranded — there is nowhere for it to go. Change something and the voltage is what adjusts.

Now connect a charged capacitor to an uncharged one, with the battery already removed. Three facts follow, in order:

1. The total charge is unchanged. It had nowhere else to go, so add up what you started with.
2. They end up in parallel — joined plate to plate, so they settle at one common voltage.
3. That voltage is V = Qtotal / Ctotal.

Charge is conserved. Energy is not. The stored energy always drops.

Where does it go? While the charge is rushing across, a current flows through the resistance of the connecting wires, and that current heats them. A little is radiated away as well. The loss is real, and it happens no matter how good your wires are.

Examiners love asking you to explain this, so have the sentence ready: charge is conserved but energy is not, because the transient current dissipates energy as heat in the resistance of the connecting wires.
