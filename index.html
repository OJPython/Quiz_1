Electric charge

Readings, quizzes and worked solutions · PHYS1025

1. What's inside everything

Read this first. Everything else depends on it.

Everything around you — the desk, the air, your hand — is made of tiny building blocks called atoms.

Each atom has a heavy centre called the nucleus, packed with particles called protons. Protons carry a positive charge, and they are stuck. They never leave.

Around the outside are much lighter particles called electrons. They carry a negative charge, and some of them are loose enough to move away.

Normally an atom has the same number of each, so the positive and negative cancel out. We call that neutral.

When something becomes charged, only one thing has happened: electrons moved. Nothing was created and nothing was destroyed.

Notice what neutral does not mean. A neutral object is not empty of charge. It is absolutely packed with charge — there is just exactly as much positive as negative, so the two cancel.

2. Charges push and pull

Charges do not touch each other, but they still feel each other across empty space.

The rule is short: the same signs push apart, opposite signs pull together.

Like charges repel. Opposite charges attract.

This is why a positively charged rod pulls electrons out of a neutral object when you touch them together — the positive rod is attracting the negative electrons.

3. Numbers in physics

The part that costs the most marks. Worth twenty minutes.

Physics uses very big and very small numbers, so we write them in a short form.

10−9 simply means divide by a thousand million. Rather than write that out every time, we put a letter in front of the unit.

So 12 nC means 12 × 10−9 C. The letter is doing all the work.

Change everything into metres and coulombs before you put any numbers into a formula. Not after. Not halfway.

4. The three ways to charge something

All three do the same thing — move electrons. They differ only in how.

By rubbing

Rub two materials together and electrons are scraped from one onto the other. One ends up short of electrons (positive), the other ends up with extra (negative).

By conduction — they touch

A charged object touches a neutral one. Electrons move across the contact until the charge is shared. Both objects end up with the same sign.

By induction — no touching

Hold a charged object close without touching. Electrons inside the neutral object shift, so one end goes negative and the other positive. The object as a whole is still neutral — nothing has entered or left it.

Charged by rubbing or induction → the two objects end up with opposite signs. Charged by conduction → they end up with the same sign.

5. One charge, one arrow

The building block. Everything else is this, repeated.

A single charge fills the space around it with an electric field. At any point, the field is an arrow — it has a size and a direction.

The size comes from one formula:

E = kQ / r2   with k = 9.0 × 109

Use the size of the charge here. Ignore the minus sign on a negative charge — it does a different job.

The sign decides the direction instead:

A worked number. What is the field 2.0 cm from a charge of +3.0 nC?

Convert first: r = 0.020 m, so r2 = 4.0 × 10−4 m2, and Q = 3.0 × 10−9 C.

E = (9.0 × 109)(3.0 × 10−9) / (4.0 × 10−4) = 6.8 × 104 N/C, pointing away from the charge.

6. Two charges on a line

With two charges you work out each field separately, then combine them. On a straight line that is easy — the two arrows either point the same way or opposite ways.

Here is the part that catches people out:

Opposite charges make the fields add between them. Like charges make them subtract. It feels backwards, so check it on the picture every time.

The reason is simple once you see it. Between a + and a −, "away from the positive" and "toward the negative" point the same way. Between two positives, each field points away from its own charge — so they fight.

Where the field is zero

Between two like charges there is a point where the two arrows are exactly equal and cancel. To find it, set the two sizes equal and solve:

kQ1 / x2 = kQ2 / (d − x)2

Cancel the k, take the square root of both sides straight away, and you avoid a messy quadratic. The point always sits closer to the smaller charge — a useful check.

7. Arrows at an angle

When the charges are not in a straight line, the arrows point at angles and you cannot just add or subtract the numbers.

The trick is to replace each slanted arrow with two arrows — one across and one up — that together take you to the same place.

Then the whole problem becomes two easy sums:

Add up all the across parts. Add up all the up parts. Those two answers are your components.

Very often one of the two sums comes out as zero, because the arrangement is symmetric and the parts cancel in pairs. Spotting that saves you most of the work — and if a question is worth fewer marks than its twin, that is usually the one that cancels.

Which part is which

Measure the angle from a line you choose, then stick to it. If the angle is measured from the vertical, the up part is E cos θ and the across part is E sin θ. Mark the angle on your own diagram before choosing — the part next to the angle always takes the cosine.

8. Getting the distance right

Half the marks lost in these questions are lost here, not in the physics.

The formula needs r2, and r is the distance from the charge to the point you care about. In shapes, that distance is rarely the number printed on the diagram.

For a square of side a, the diagonal is a√2, so from a corner to the centre is half of that: a/√2. For a = 10 cm that is 0.0707 m, and r2 = 5.0 × 10−3 m2.

For an equilateral triangle of side a, every corner is a distance a from every other corner. No square roots needed.

Find r, convert it to metres, then square it. Write r2 down as its own line of working before you touch the field formula.

9. The field between two plates

A completely different kind of field from the ones in part 2 — and a much easier one.

Take two flat metal plates, face to face, and connect them to a battery. One goes positive, the other negative, and the space between them fills with field.

What makes this special: the field between the plates is the same everywhere — same size, same direction, at every point in the gap. We call that a uniform field, and we draw it as evenly spaced parallel arrows.

The size of that field comes from one short formula:

E = V / d   — volts across the gap, divided by the gap in metres

A worked number. A potential difference of 5 kV across plates 50 mm apart.

Convert both: 5 kV = 5000 V, and 50 mm = 0.050 m.

E = 5000 / 0.050 = 1.0 × 105 N/C.

Notice there is no kQ/r2 anywhere here, and no distance squared. Plates are the easy case — the moment you see two plates and a voltage, reach for V/d.

10. When a charge hangs in balance

The single most common calculation in this topic. Learn the shape of it once.

Hang a small charged ball on a thread inside a sideways field and it will not hang straight down. The field pushes it sideways, so the thread settles at an angle and stays there.

Staying there is the whole key. Nothing is moving, so all the forces must cancel. Three forces act:

Split the tension into its up part and its sideways part. Up must balance the weight, sideways must balance the electric force:

T cos θ = mg   (vertical)      T sin θ = qE   (horizontal)

Now divide the second by the first. The T cancels and disappears completely:

tan θ = qE / mg   →   q = mg tan θ / E

That cancellation is the whole trick. You are never told the tension and you never need it. Any question that hangs a charge on a thread is this one formula with different numbers.

And the direction tells you the sign: if the ball leans the same way the field points, the charge is positive. If it leans against the field, it is negative.

11. Grams, millimetres and g

Ten marks have never been lost to Gauss's law. They are lost here.

These questions hand you numbers in whatever units suit the sentence, and every formula expects kilograms, metres and volts. Convert before you start, on their own lines of working.

Mass is not weight. m is in kilograms; mg is a force in newtons. The formula wants mg.

And do not panic when a number goes unused. If a question gives a 20.0 cm string and your working never touches it, that is fine — the length would only matter if you were asked how far sideways the ball moved. Forcing every number into the answer is how you end up with nonsense.

12. Gauss's law

This is bookwork. It is written out the same way every year, so learn the words and the picture.

Picture any closed surface — a bag, a box, a balloon — sitting in an electric field. Flux is a measure of how much field passes out through that surface.

Gauss's law says something remarkable: the total flux out depends on nothing but the charge inside. Not the shape of the surface, not its size, not where the charge sits inside it.

Φ = ∮ E · dA = Qenclosed / ε0   with ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12

By itself that looks useless, because the integral is frightening. The power comes from choosing the surface cleverly.

For a single point charge, choose a sphere centred on the charge. Two things then become true at once, and they are the two things the examiner wants you to say:

• By symmetry, E has the same size at every point on that sphere.
• E points straight out, so it is perpendicular to the surface everywhere.

Because E is constant and perpendicular, the integral collapses into a multiplication — field times area:

∮ E · dA = E × 4πr2

Set that equal to Q/ε0 and make E the subject:

E (4πr2) = Q / ε0   →   E = Q / (4πε0r2) = kQ / r2

And out drops Coulomb's law. The k you have been using all along is just a shorthand: k = 1/(4πε0) = 9.0 × 109.

13. What a capacitor stores

The biggest topic on the paper. More marks ride on these four readings than on anything else in the course.

A capacitor is just two conductors with a gap between them. Push charge onto one and an equal, opposite charge appears on the other, and a potential difference builds up across the gap.

How much charge it holds per volt is its capacitance:

C = Q / V   — measured in farads (F)

A farad is enormous, so real capacitors come in microfarads (µF, 10−6), nanofarads (nF, 10−9) and picofarads (pF, 10−12).

Here is the part that trips people. C does not depend on Q or V. Double the charge and the voltage doubles too, leaving C unchanged. Capacitance is fixed by the shape of the thing — how big the plates are and how far apart.

Bigger plates → more capacitance. Closer plates → more capacitance. A dielectric in the gap → K times more capacitance.

ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12, the same constant from Gauss's law. The dielectric constant K is just a number — 4 means four times the capacitance of the same capacitor with air in it.

14. Series and parallel

Two rules, and one thing to remember about each.

Everything in a capacitor network comes from asking a single question about each pair: what do they share?

Capacitors are backwards from resistors. Resistors add in series; capacitors add in parallel.

That is not an arbitrary fact to memorise. Two capacitors side by side in parallel are literally one capacitor with twice the plate area, and more area means more capacitance. Two in series make the total gap wider, and a wider gap means less.

When one capacitor is really two in parallel

Slide a slab of dielectric in so it covers only part of the plate area, but fills the gap from top to bottom. You now have two capacitors side by side — and because both pieces sit between the same two plates, both have the same p.d. across them.

Same voltage means parallel. Work out each piece from its own share of the area, then add.

Watch the trap: the slab's thickness here equals the full gap, so it is not splitting the capacitor top-from-bottom. It is splitting it side-by-side, by area. Check which one a question means before you start.

15. Energy in a capacitor

Charging a capacitor takes work. You are pushing charge onto a plate that is already charged and pushing back, so each extra bit costs more than the last. That work is stored in the field between the plates, and you get it back when it discharges.

Because Q and V rise together in a straight line, the stored energy is the triangle underneath — half of Q times V, not the whole rectangle. Substituting Q = CV gives three versions of the same formula:

U = ½QV = ½CV2 = Q2 / 2C

They always give the same answer, so pick whichever matches the two things you already know. Know C and V? Use ½CV2. Know Q and C — which is the usual case after charge has been shared around? Use Q2/2C.

The ½ is the most commonly dropped symbol on the whole paper. It costs a mark every time.

16. Sharing charge when the battery is removed

Read this one twice. It is worth more marks than anything else in part 4.

Everything hangs on one question: is the battery still connected?

• Battery connected. The battery holds the voltage fixed. Change the capacitor and the charge is what adjusts.
• Battery removed. The charge is now stranded — there is nowhere for it to go. Change something and the voltage is what adjusts.

Now connect a charged capacitor to an uncharged one, with the battery already removed. Three facts follow, in order:

1. The total charge is unchanged. It had nowhere else to go, so add up what you started with.
2. They end up in parallel — joined plate to plate, so they settle at one common voltage.
3. That voltage is V = Qtotal / Ctotal.

Charge is conserved. Energy is not. The stored energy always drops.

Where does it go? While the charge is rushing across, a current flows through the resistance of the connecting wires, and that current heats them. A little is radiated away as well. The loss is real, and it happens no matter how good your wires are.

Examiners love asking you to explain this, so have the sentence ready: charge is conserved but energy is not, because the transient current dissipates energy as heat in the resistance of the connecting wires.

17. What a current actually is

Everything so far has been charges sitting still. From here they move.

An electric current is simply flowing charge. Put a number on it by counting how much charge passes a point each second:

I = q / t   — measured in amperes (A), and 1 A = 1 coulomb per second

Charge has to have somewhere to go and somewhere to come back from, so nothing flows unless there is a complete circuit — an unbroken conducting loop from one terminal of the battery, through the device, and back to the other terminal. One wire is never enough.

And nothing is used up on the way round. A battery does not create charge and a bulb does not swallow it. The same charge goes round and round; what the battery supplies is energy, and what the bulb takes is energy.

By convention, current is drawn flowing from + to −, the way a positive charge would go. The electrons actually move the other way. Both pictures describe the same thing — use the conventional one.

That convention is a historical accident: the direction was chosen before anyone knew electrons existed. Nothing breaks because of it, so long as you stay consistent.

18. Ohm's law

Push harder on the charges and more of them flow. Ohm found that for a metal conductor the current is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, and the constant linking them is the resistance:

V = I R   — resistance in ohms (Ω), and 1 Ω = 1 V/A

Resistance is a hindrance to flow. It comes from the moving electrons colliding with the atoms of the material, which is also why a resistor gets warm.

Plot current against voltage and an ordinary resistor gives a straight line through the origin — that is what ohmic means. A light-bulb filament does not, because it heats up as the current rises and its resistance climbs with it.

Doubling the voltage doubles the current. It does not change the resistance — resistance is a property of the object.

That last point is worth pausing on, because it is the most common exam trap in this topic. V = IR does not say that R depends on V. It says that whatever V you apply, the current adjusts so that the ratio V/I stays the same.

19. What a wire's resistance depends on

Resistance is not something a material simply has — it depends on the shape of the piece you cut. Three things set it:

R = ρ l / A   — resistivity × length, divided by cross-sectional area

Longer means more resistance, because the electrons have further to barge through. Fatter means less, because there are more lanes side by side. And ρ (rho), the resistivity, is the part that belongs to the material itself.

Look at the span in that bottom row. Copper and hard rubber differ by about twenty powers of ten — which is why one is wire and the other is the sleeve around it.

Temperature

Heat a metal and its atoms vibrate harder, so the electrons collide more often and the resistivity goes up:

ρT = ρ0 [1 + α(T − T0)]

For a metal α is positive. For a semiconductor it can be negative, because heating frees more electrons to carry current and that effect wins. That is exactly the thermistor from part 1.

20. Resistors in series and parallel

Read this beside reading 14. The rules are the mirror image of the capacitor ones, and mixing them up is costly.

Same question as before: what do they share?

Resistors add in series. Capacitors add in parallel. The two sets of rules are swapped, so always ask yourself which component you are looking at before you reach for a formula.

The sense of it: two resistors in a row make a longer obstacle course, so more resistance. Two side by side open a second lane, so less. Adding a parallel resistor always brings the total below the smallest one you started with — a free check on your answer.

A worked one (2024 A4). Three resistors of 750 Ω, 680 Ω and 990 Ω all in parallel:

1/R = 1/750 + 1/680 + 1/990 = 0.003814, so R = 262 Ω.

Below 680, as it has to be. The commonest mistake here is stopping at 0.003814 and writing that down — you must flip it at the end.

21. Power, energy and the bill

Each coulomb of charge that falls through a potential difference V gives up energy qV. Divide by time and you have the rate — the power:

P = I V   and, using V = IR,   P = I2R = V2/R   (watts)

Three forms of one formula, like the capacitor energy ones. Pick whichever matches the two quantities you already know.

That bottom box catches people every year. “Watt” appears in the name, so it looks like a power unit — but it is a power multiplied by a time, which makes it energy. It is what the meter on the wall counts and what the municipality charges you for.

Why the pylons run at hundreds of thousands of volts

Transmission lines have resistance, and the power wasted heating them is I2R. Since the loss goes as the current squared, the way to cut it is to send the same power at a much higher voltage so that far less current flows. Raise V by a hundred and the current drops by a hundred, and the loss drops by ten thousand. You will prove exactly this in the quiz.

Fuses and overloading

Household appliances are wired in parallel, so each gets the full mains voltage. But every extra appliance opens another path, so the total current climbs. Too much current and the wiring in the wall gets hot enough to start a fire — the circuit is overloaded. A fuse or circuit breaker sits in series with the supply and opens when the current passes a safe value.

A note on AC

Mains electricity is alternating current: it reverses direction many times a second rather than flowing steadily one way. Its voltage swings between +V0 and −V0, so quoting the peak would overstate what it does. Instead we quote the rms value, which is the steady DC value that would deliver the same power:

Irms = I0/√2 = 0.707 I0   and   Vrms = V0/√2

When a plug says 230 V, that is the rms value — the peak is actually about 325 V. Average power is then simply P̄ = IrmsVrms.

22. Real batteries

Part 5A treated batteries as perfect. They are not, and the difference is worth marks.

A battery turns chemical energy into electrical energy. The energy it gives each coulomb is its emf, written ℰ — and you met it in part 1 as the voltage across the terminals when no current is flowing.

The moment current does flow, the charge has to fight its way through the chemicals inside the battery itself. That internal obstruction is the internal resistance r, and it swallows some of the voltage before it ever reaches your circuit.

Vab = ℰ − I r   — the terminal voltage, which is always less than the emf once current flows

Two consequences worth holding on to. With no current, Ir is zero and the terminal voltage equals the emf exactly — which is why the definition says “no current flowing”. And the bigger the current, the more voltage is lost inside, which is why your headlights dim the instant the starter motor turns over.

The circuit equation

Put the battery across an external resistance R and all the energy has to go somewhere — some into R, the rest wasted inside r:

ℰ = I r + I R   so   I = ℰ / (R + r)

The internal resistance is just one more resistance in series. Forgetting to add it is the single most common slip in these questions.

23. When series and parallel run out

In part 5A every circuit could be untangled by spotting what was in series and what was in parallel, collapsing it down, and using Ohm's law. Plenty of circuits cannot be untangled that way at all.

Put two batteries in different branches, for instance, and no two resistors are simply in series or in parallel any more. For those you need Kirchhoff's rules — two statements that are really just conservation of charge and conservation of energy, dressed for circuits.

The junction rule. At any point where wires meet, everything that arrives must leave. Charge does not pile up at a junction.

The loop rule. Walk all the way round any closed loop and come back to where you started, and your potential must be back where it started too. So every rise and every drop around the loop must cancel.

Junction rule → charge is conserved. Loop rule → energy is conserved. Everything else in this topic is bookkeeping.

The loop rule is the one people find strange. The sense of it: potential is like height on a hill. Walk a circular path and however much you climb, you must descend the same amount by the time you are back at the start.

24. Signs: walking round a loop

This is where the marks are actually lost. Not the physics — the plus and minus signs.

Applying the loop rule means walking round the circuit adding up every change in potential. There are exactly four situations, and you need all four:

A resistor only cares which way the current runs relative to your walk. A cell only cares which way its terminals face relative to your walk. The cell does not care about the current at all.

That last sentence is the one to memorise. People lose marks by flipping a cell's sign because the current runs backwards through it — but a battery being charged still gains you ℰ when you walk from its − to its +, no matter which way the current is going.

25. The recipe

Kirchhoff problems look frightening and are actually mechanical. Follow the same five steps every time and the only hard part is the algebra at the end.

You are allowed to guess wrong

Step 1 says guess the direction of each current freely, and that is not sloppiness — it is the method working as designed. If you guess wrong, the algebra hands you a negative answer, which means precisely: right size, opposite direction to my arrow.

A negative current is not a mistake. It is the arithmetic telling you to turn your arrow round. Quote the magnitude and state the real direction.

The slides' own worked example ends with I₁ = −0.87 A for exactly this reason, and that negative sign is part of the right answer.

A shortcut worth knowing

When every branch runs between the same two nodes — which happens more often than you would think — you can skip the loop equations. Call the potential difference between those two nodes V, write the current in each branch in terms of V, and make them add to zero at one node. One equation, one unknown. Two of the four problems in this part work that way.

26. Meters, and capacitors that take time

Ammeters and voltmeters

Two instruments, and the difference between them is entirely about where they go and what resistance they must have.

An ammeter goes in series and needs near-zero resistance, so it does not reduce the current it is measuring. A voltmeter goes in parallel and needs enormous resistance, so it does not steal current from the branch.

Both rules come from the same idea: a meter must not change the thing it is measuring.

RC circuits

Put a resistor and a capacitor in series and close the switch, and the capacitor does not charge instantly. The resistor throttles the current, so the voltage climbs steeply at first and then levels off:

VC = ℰ(1 − e−t/RC)   and the time constant is τ = R C

τ is how long the capacitor takes to reach about 63% of its final voltage. Bigger R or bigger C means slower charging — and because the units work out to seconds, checking that is a quick way to catch an error.

The current does the opposite: it starts at ℰ/R the instant the switch closes and dies away exponentially, I = (ℰ/R)e−t/RC, because as the capacitor fills it pushes back harder.

Miscellanea — side topics that sit underneath the exam questions rather than inside any one of them. First up: vectors, which is what adding electric fields actually is.

A number, and a number with a direction

Start here. Five minutes, and a lot of the rest stops being mysterious.

Some quantities in physics are just a number with a unit. A mass of 2 kg. A charge of 5 nC. An energy of 0.16 J. Nothing more to say about them — we call these scalars.

Others make no sense until you also say which way. A force of 10 N — pushing where? An electric field of 50 N/C — pointing where? These are vectors, and we draw them as arrows.

Charge, mass, energy, potential and capacitance are scalars. Force, electric field and velocity are vectors.

That split explains something you have already met. Potential V is a scalar, so to combine potentials you just add the numbers. Field E is a vector, so to combine fields you have to deal with directions — which is the entire reason the field questions are harder than the potential ones.

Why you cannot just add them

Two forces, each 50 N. What is the total?

The honest answer is: it depends entirely on the directions, and the possible answers run all the way from 100 down to 0.

You never add the lengths of two vectors. You add their parts, direction by direction.

Nose to tail

There is a way to see the answer without any arithmetic. Draw the first arrow. Then start the second arrow where the first one ended, keeping its direction exactly. The resultant runs from the very start to the very finish.

This is worth drawing roughly in the margin even when you intend to calculate. It tells you instantly whether the answer should point up or down, left or right — so when your arithmetic produces something pointing the other way, you catch it.

Sketch it nose to tail first. Then calculate. The sketch is your check.

Breaking one arrow into two

Drawing is fine for two arrows and hopeless for four. So we do the opposite of adding: we take each slanted arrow apart into an across piece and an up piece.

Think of it as giving directions. Instead of “go diagonally that way for 50 m”, you say “go 40 m east, then 30 m north”. You arrive at exactly the same place.

Those two pieces are called the components, written Ax for the across one and Ay for the up one. The arrow and its two components always form a right-angled triangle, which is why Pythagoras keeps appearing.

Ax and Ay together carry everything the arrow did. Once you have them, you can forget the arrow.

Which one takes the cosine

The step most people get wrong, and it comes down to one habit.

To get the components you need the angle — and the formulas flip depending on where you measured the angle from. That is the whole difficulty.

Do not memorise two sets of formulas. Memorise one sentence:

The side next to the angle takes the cos. The side opposite the angle takes the sin.

So the habit is: draw the arrow, mark the angle on your own diagram, see which side touches it, and only then write a formula. That is why the equilateral-triangle question uses cos 30° for the upward part — the angle there is measured from the vertical, so the vertical side is the one next to it.

Adding by components

Now the whole method, and it is only four steps. It works for two vectors or for twenty.

A worked one. A = (40, 30) and B = (−30, 40).

Rx = 40 + (−30) = 10     Ry = 30 + 40 = 70

So R = (10, 70). Note what did not happen: 50 and 50 did not become 100.

Keep the x column and the y column physically apart on the page. Two separate lines of working, never one.

Very often one of the two columns adds up to exactly zero, because the arrangement is symmetric. Spotting that early saves you most of the work — and if one part of a question carries fewer marks than its twin, that is usually the one that cancels.

Back to a size and a direction

Components are how you calculate, but an answer usually has to come back out as a size and a direction. The right-angled triangle gives you both.

|R| = √(Rx2 + Ry2)   and   θ = tan−1(Ry / Rx)

One warning about that second formula. A calculator only ever returns an angle between −90° and +90°, so if your Rx came out negative the arrow actually points into the left half of the page and the true angle is the calculator's answer plus 180°.

This is exactly why the nose-to-tail sketch matters. Look at your sketch, see roughly where the resultant should point, and if the calculator disagrees, trust the picture and fix the angle.
